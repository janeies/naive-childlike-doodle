# Style System

Use this reference when deciding how to translate a subject into the naive childlike doodle style or when diagnosing why a result feels too polished, busy, or detached from the scene.

## Line language

- Prefer uneven felt-tip, crayon, dry-brush, or soft ink marks.
- Let long edges bend slightly and let repeated elements differ from one another.
- Keep corners soft, blunt, or inconsistently joined rather than mechanically precise.
- Use thicker outer contours and fewer, lighter interior marks when hierarchy is needed.
- Do not clean every wobble. Controlled imperfection is part of the identity.

## Shape and proportion

- Build forms from a small number of broad shapes.
- Slightly exaggerate the most recognizable feature while keeping the rest simple.
- Allow mild asymmetry in eyes, ears, limbs, handles, windows, furniture, and architecture.
- Keep perspective readable but relaxed; avoid technically perfect vanishing points unless the user explicitly needs them.

## Detail density

- Preserve details that identify the subject or explain the action.
- Remove repeated micro-texture, tiny architectural lines, unnecessary seams, and photographic surface detail.
- As a practical target, a redraw may retain roughly one third to one half of the source's secondary details while keeping all identity anchors.

## Color and tone

- Default paper: warm off-white rather than bright digital white.
- Default ink: near-black rather than fully neutral black when a softer print feeling helps.
- Use two or three neutral values: light, medium, and occasional dark.
- Accent colors should be muted, slightly dusty, and limited to focal characters or products.
- Avoid neon colors, smooth airbrushed shading, glossy highlights, and cinematic color grading unless requested.

## Halftone and texture

- Use halftone to describe a wall plane, fabric panel, shadow, or depth change.
- Vary density rather than adding more colors.
- Leave faces and important small features mostly free of dense dots.
- Keep large quiet areas untextured so the illustration can breathe.

## Characters

- Preserve the signature head silhouette, eye construction, markings, expression, and key accessory.
- Keep limbs and paws simple; avoid anatomical realism.
- Ground the character with a small contact shadow, irregular line, or sparse dots when the figure otherwise looks pasted onto the scene.
- A stern, shy, sleepy, or awkward expression should remain legible after simplification.

## Scenes

- Establish place with two to five cues, not a complete inventory.
- Outdoor examples: curb line, lamp, two road marks, snow edge, one building mass.
- Indoor examples: sofa contour, window, lamp, rug edge, one or two objects.
- Background lines should usually be thinner, lighter, or less dense than the subject.

## Products and cutouts

- Preserve construction, openings, handles, closures, motifs, and attachment points.
- Complete cropped or occluded parts only when requested; keep the reconstruction plausible and symmetric only to the degree the handmade object requires.
- For transparent assets, remove the background and cast shadow unless the user explicitly wants a separate natural shadow.

## Common corrections

- **Too straight or vector-like:** increase line wobble, pressure variation, blunt joins, and asymmetry.
- **Too detailed:** remove secondary architecture, fabric micro-detail, decorative marks, and repeated background objects.
- **Too gray or busy:** clear texture from negative space and keep halftone only on structural planes.
- **Colors too loud:** reduce saturation and contrast; limit accents to one or two focal regions.
- **Character looks pasted on:** add a restrained contact shadow or ground line and match the character's tonal contrast to the scene.
- **Looks like polished children's-book art:** reduce smooth shading, perfect curves, cute decorative fillers, and professional vector regularity.
- **Identity drift:** restate the exact facial markers, proportions, pose, and accessories from the reference; do not ask for a broad reinterpretation.
